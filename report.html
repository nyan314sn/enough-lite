2. Motivation

Take a couple, 45 and 43. Three children, two dogs, one income of $380,000, $750,000 saved. They ask one question: can we stop work at 62? They have three choices today. They can pay an adviser: a flat-fee plan averages $2,926 and a yearly retainer $6,815 (Envestnet, Q1 2026). They can fill in a planner that asks for a hundred inputs and speaks in jargon. Or they can ask a chatbot and hope the numbers are right.

Most people do none of these. Only 35% of non-retirees say their retirement saving is on track (Federal Reserve, SHED 2025). The median worker aged 21 to 64 has $955 saved; among workers who have any savings, $40,000 (NIRS, 2026). Only 32% of people who sought financial guidance used a professional adviser (Gallup and Edward Jones, 2026).

What does the gap cost? Money, if they pay. Time and nerve, if they plan alone. Peace of mind, if they do nothing. Enough Lite answers the one question, in plain words, in about ten seconds of compute and a few minutes of typing, for about $0.002 a plan (section 5).

What I claim. One AI assistant over a planning engine exists (Boldin, Copilot). A multi-agent AI adviser exists (Origin). What I could find in none of them (I say "could find", not "proved none exists"):

  1. An answer shaped as needs, wants, wishes. Needs are safe: housing, food, health care and the pets, paid in nearly every market path. Wants are sure: the spending you can count on in 90 of 100 paths. Wishes stay open: nice if markets help, not part of the plan.
  2. Children and pets in plain words, with costs by year (college, dogs aging out), a "family moments" list and a chart of costs now and later (mortgage paid off, children leave, Medicare starts).
  3. Numbers only from tools. The language model never makes up a figure. A number check rejects any figure that no tool produced, and I measured how often it fires (section 5).
  4. Strategies from households like yours. Markets tests the whole plans of the five most similar households, and a rule of thumb, on the person's own market scenarios and keeps the best (the appendix).
  5. A cost of cents per plan, with hard dollar caps, and a team you can watch: each hand-off shows on screen as "Team talk".
  6. Checked numbers inside any AI assistant. An optional MCP server lets an assistant call the same engine, sources and refusals the app uses, instead of guessing.

What I lack. No linked accounts. No regulation or human behind the advice. No independent test of accuracy. The advice is not financial advice.

4. The agents

Enough Lite has one lead adviser and six specialists. Each specialist has one job and plain-Python tools. The tools do the arithmetic. The model reads their output and writes it up in plain words. Each tool call streams to the screen as it happens, so the user can watch the team work.

Each field has an inline editor, each section a Reset, and "Start over" clears all.

Who hands work to whom. The lead adviser starts the specialists and gives each a question. Longevity, Family and Tax hand their findings to Markets, so the simulation uses their figures. Markets and the others hand findings back to the lead, who writes the plan. Every message is built from tool output and shows in the "Team talk" log.

Model, and why. Two providers, chosen in one place (app/config.py). With ANTHROPIC_API_KEY set, all seven agents use Claude Haiku 5.5 (claude-haiku-5-5), the default. The course Portkey key given to me was rejected ("Invalid API Key"), so the app calls the providers directly. The model only reads tool output and writes short plain text, so a cheap fast model is enough. Haiku price: $0.10 per million input tokens, $0.50 per million output, cache reads $0.01. I ran both models on the same 80 households before choosing Haiku (section 5). Every cost and eval in this report is measured on Haiku, paid with my own key, except that comparison. Without a key the app still runs and uses plain template text.

What is AI and what is not

Enough Lite is not "an AI that plans your retirement". The AI handles words; code handles money.

Part of the work Done by How
Read what the person types Rules first, AI only if the rules fail A rule parser fills most fields; the model is called for the rest, and every guess is shown for approval
Tax, Social Security, life expectancy, family and pet costs Code Plain-Python tools with published tables (IRS, SSA, life tables, BLS spending)
Will the money last? Code 2,000 market paths for each plan (stocks 5.0% real a year, spread 17%; bonds 1.8%, spread 6%)
Which strategy (stock share by age, when to claim Social Security, an income annuity) Code, using plans solved ahead of time See below
The plan in plain words, the three actions, Team talk AI Writes only from tool output; the number check rejects any figure no tool produced
Rule questions (Medicare, 401(k) limits) AI over cited passages Answers only from 37 official passages, or says it has no source

Plans solved ahead of time, not learning. The full version of this app had an optimizer that, for each household, searched strategies (stock share by stage of life, Social Security claiming age, income annuities) over many simulated markets. That search is slow, so I ran it ahead of time on synthetic households and kept 467 solved plans (app/data/households.json). For a new person, Markets finds the 5 most similar households (19 weighted features) and re-tests each one's whole strategy on the person's own numbers; the person's retirement age is never changed. The AI does not learn from the optimizer: no training, no fine-tuning, a lookup of solved cases. In a replay of 60 households, copying the best neighbour's strategy reached a median 100% of the optimizer's spending (10th percentile 97.7%), against 92.1% for the rule of thumb. When no neighbour is close, or the best plan pays the basics in fewer than 95 of 100 paths, the plan says it is low-confidence.

Simulating, not optimizing. Enough Lite simulates; it does not optimize. For each person it simulates 2,000 market paths for about twenty candidate strategies (five borrowed from the solved households, the rule of thumb and its variants: a few points more or less in stocks, claiming Social Security at retirement; the team panel shows the count, 21 for the example in this report), keeps the best, and finds the highest spending that lasts in 90 of 100 paths. Pre-optimized plans play two roles: at run time they supply candidates that are re-tested on each person's numbers; offline they are the benchmark that validates the engine (median gap −0.8%, in the appendix). Separately, every figure the AI writes is checked against the tools' output; a mismatch sends the AI back once to correct itself, then falls back to a template.

What the plan considers. One average future would hide the risk, so every plan is run across many:

  • Markets. 2,000 random paths of yearly stock and bond returns after inflation (stocks 5.0% a year on average with a 17% spread, bonds 1.8% with 6%, correlated 0.1), with a stock share that falls with age. The plan reports what happens in the middle path and in the bad ones: "count on" is the spending that lasts in 90 of 100.
  • Bad markets on purpose. The analysis reruns the plan under one stress at a time: a poor first decade of retirement (stocks earn 5 points a year less for 10 years) and higher inflation (1.5 points a year more for 10 years, so fixed pensions and annuities buy less). What If adds a 30% fall now or at retirement, and 3 points of extra inflation.
  • How long you live. A U.S. mortality table (SOA 2024, with yearly improvement), adjusted for sex and health, sets the planning age: the age by which there is only a 1 in 4 chance that anyone in the household is still alive. For couples, the first death is placed at life expectancy: from then on Social Security steps down to the survivor's benefit and spending falls to 75%.
  • Living longer or shorter than expected. The analysis tests living to 100 (each person in a couple) and, for couples, the first death coming 10 years early. Each test says whether the basics are still paid and, if not, for how many years they fall short.

Lifespan is set per household, not drawn at random in each market path; the stress tests cover the long and short cases.

The harness: how the agents are kept under control

The model never acts on its own. Everything around it is code.

  • Tools. Each agent can call only its own plain-Python tools. Tools return typed data; agents return typed objects (headline, details, numbers), not free text.
  • Stopping rules. Each specialist: at most 4 model requests, 24,000 input and 4,000 output tokens (lead adviser: 4 requests, 8,000 output). Timeouts: 30 s a request, 60 s an agent, 12 s for a specialist's writing, 24 s of AI time a plan. Past any limit the agent stops and a template built from the tools' numbers is used, so a plan never hangs or loops.
  • Spending caps. Before each call the app checks the worst case against $0.05 a plan, $0.50 a session and $5.00 for the server. Plans are limited to 6 a minute and 60 a day per address.
  • Checks on the input. Emergencies go to 911 or 988 before any model call. Injection patterns, requests to hide assets for Medicaid or SSI, and single-stock or crypto picks are refused (section 6).
  • The number check. Every figure in the lead adviser's and each specialist's text must appear in a tool output or the profile. A failed figure gets one retry with the rejected figures named; a second failure uses the template. It also rejects machine words such as "the tools". The person never sees an unchecked figure (section 5 shows what tuning it did).
  • Prompt caching. Fixed instructions, tool list and profile come first in each prompt and are marked cacheable; later calls read them at a tenth of the input price (measured in section 5).
  • A log. Every call writes tokens, cost, time and outcome to runs/usage.jsonl; the app shows each plan's tokens and cost under Team talk.

The MCP server: Enough's numbers for any AI assistant

Ask a chatbot about retirement and it often makes up the numbers. mcp_server.py is an optional server (Model Context Protocol) that lets an assistant such as Claude Desktop or Claude Code call Enough's own engine and sources instead. No AI model runs inside it: it is plain code with the same engine, the same seed (409) and the same refusals as the app, and nothing sent to it is stored or logged. The web app does not need it. To add it: pip install -r requirements-mcp.txt, then point the assistant's config at python mcp_server.py (README, "Use Enough from your AI assistant (MCP)"). It has 6 tools and 33 tests (tests/test_mcp_server.py), which include the refusals and the forbidden-port check.

Step 3. The plan. The headline says: "You can count on $114,800 a year: $44,500 for basics and $70,300 for the rest." Needs: the basics are paid in 100 of 100 scenarios. Wants: $114,800 a year in 90 of 100. Wishes: the goal of $114,800 is met in 90 of 100, and costs fall by $22,500 a year when the mortgage ends in 2045. What I did to keep cost down

  1. Tools do the maths. Simulation, tax and life tables are Python. The model never does arithmetic.
  2. A small, fast model. Claude Haiku 5.5, not a larger one (compared below).
  3. A rule parser first for intake. Most messages cost nothing; only the ones the rules cannot read call the model.
  4. Short, typed outputs with a cap on output tokens.
  5. Prompt caching. Two thirds of input tokens are cache reads at a tenth of the price.
  6. Fewer retries. The number-check tuning below cut retries and template fallbacks to zero on ten test households, and the cost of a plan fell with them.
  7. Hard caps per agent, per run ($0.05), per session ($0.50) and in total ($5.00). Section 6 shows a cap working.

6. Safety

The risks that matter for this app are specific. People paste real financial statements, so hidden instructions in a document matter. The app gives money advice, so invented numbers and advice to hide assets from Medicaid matter. It costs money per call, so overspending matters. It is a web app that takes text and rebuilds a plan from a profile the browser sends back, so forged requests, slow-regex attacks, oversized input and script injection matter.

Defenses, in layers

  1. Untrusted input is fenced. Typed or pasted text is wrapped as data. A guard strips hidden text (zero-width characters, comments, hidden markup), flags instruction-like phrases and fake role labels (SYSTEM:), and removes fake closing tags.
  2. Range checks. Age, retirement age, savings, children and pets are checked against plausible ranges. A poisoned value is not stored; the app asks what was meant.
  3. Numbers come from tools only. The number check (section 4) also guards against an agent that invents a fact.
  4. Refusals, by rule before the model runs: hiding assets for Medicaid or SSI, falsifying balances. Emergencies get a pointer to emergency help.
  5. No tool can act. Tools read and compute; no agent can send email, spend money or change files.
  6. Loops end, and the numbers still show. Call, token and time limits; if the AI fails or the budget runs out, the tools' numbers are shown.
  7. Web hardening. A body-size limit, a shape check on every incoming profile, a CSP and other headers, same-origin only, safe logs, rate limits.

Prompt-injection tests: 45 of 50 at first, 74 of 74 after the fixes

I wrote 50 attacks in 9 categories, then added 24 for the newest paths (plan-chat corrections, forged pending changes, split answers, assumption chips, multi-turn attacks, script injection). A script (safety_tests/run_attacks.py) sends each to the running app and judges the reply by fixed rules: no planted canary word, no key or prompt leak, no invented dollar figure, a refusal where one is due, no server error, spend inside the cap.

7. Impact

Who this helps, and the size of the problem

The figures on the size of the problem (Federal Reserve SHED 2025: 35% of non-retirees on track; NIRS 2026: median savings $955; Gallup and Edward Jones 2026: 32% of guidance-seekers used an adviser; Envestnet Q1 2026: a flat-fee plan averages $2,926) and their links are in section 2. Two cautions: NIRS is an advocacy group using Census data from 2022; Gallup's page counts the 18% who sought guidance from AI among guidance-seekers, while press reports say all adults.

Cost to the person. Against a $2,926 flat-fee plan, one plan from Enough Lite costs about $0.002 and a session about $0.01 (section 5). That is not a like-for-like swap. An adviser covers tax planning, estate and judgment that this app does not. The honest claim is access: a first answer for people who would not pay for any plan.

Quality. Simulated people, not real ones: on the 40-person test the intake reached a plan every time and got 96% of fields right, from 75% (appendix). Treat this as a test of the intake, not as proof of impact.

Time. The agents take a median of about 9 seconds a plan on a quiet machine (20 plans in a row, no template fallbacks). With three plans at once the median was 11.0 s and the 90th percentile 32.6 s. How long a person takes to type their household is for the user test below.

8. Limits and next steps

What does not work yet

  • Plan quality is not proved. The intake is measured (95% of fields correct on 48 simulated people), and the engine matches an independent recompute. Plan quality is not: the gap to the optimizer is near zero in the middle but wide at the edges, verdict agreement is 85%, and the AI judge called more plans wrong after the first fixes, not fewer. I claim no pass rate for plan quality.
  • Not tested by an independent person. The auditors are AI agents and the testers are simulated. The real-user evidence is one or two people at most.
  • The app is a plan, not an account. No linked accounts, no trading, no human, not regulated. Every screen says it is not financial advice.
  • Known gaps in the engine (reasonableness audit, R1 to R10, not changed): R1 pre-retirement shortfalls are dropped (28 of 299 plans do not respond to savings plus or minus 50%); R2 a retiree's mortgage sits inside spending (adding $300,000 of mortgage raised one plan's count-on from $111,300 to $164,800); R3 a plan that falls short maximises count-on, which can trade away basics; R4 a What If keeps the plan's strategy while basics are safe, up to 15% below a fresh plan; R5 a partner's Social Security that starts before you retire is ignored; R6 retiring later can lower count-on in falls-short plans; R7 withdrawal tax is measured at the goal (up to 3% in count-on); R8 a mortgage payment below typical frees nothing when it ends; R9 debts paid from pre-tax savings are untaxed; R10 fixed payments stay level in today's dollars.
  • Simple models. A 2,000-path simulation, a mortality table and flat tax rates. No Roth conversion search, no annuity design, no business income.
  • Estimates for skipped inputs. The app proposes a figure and asks for approval. It is still a guess.
  • One display gap. In Compare, the "goal lasts in" cell for the 30% market fall shows a dash, though the tab shows 82 of 100.
  • Provider. The default model runs on my own Anthropic key. With the staff's OPENAI_API_KEY the app runs gpt-6-luna: same numbers, but slower plans and more template wording (section 5).
  • The 10-minute rule. A dry run on 2026-11-21 copied the repo to a new folder outside OneDrive, put .env one level up and followed the README: creating the virtual environment took 8 s, pip install 80 s and starting the server under 1 s, about 1.5 minutes in all. The first plan then ran in 23 s. [[TODO: repeat once from the final GitHub clone.]]

What I would build next, given another month

  1. Run the user test with five people and fix what confuses them.
  2. Let a person upload a statement (the injection defense is built for it).
  3. Fill the gaps the full optimizer covers: Roth conversion, long-term care design, annuity choice; and fix R1 to R10.
  4. Compare Haiku with a larger Claude model on the same 300 households, and gpt-6-luna with looser time caps.
  5. Let a couple answer together, each on a phone.
  6. Have a planner review 30 plans and measure where the app and the planner differ.
  7. Put the app online with a monthly budget and a public cost page.

Appendix: how we tested and improved it

I did not trust my own checks. After the app worked, I ran a loop: independent auditors try to break it, I fix what they find, and I measure again. The auditors are separate Claude agents that did not build the app. Each had its own copy, wrote down what it saw, and rated each finding P0 (a person gets a wrong or dead-end answer), P1 (a clear fault) or P2 (polish). The log is docs/LOOP_LOG.md.

Three rounds of audits

Audit A recomputes the logic (tax, Social Security, costs) and runs its own simulation. Audit B walks the screens in a real browser, desktop and phone (10 journeys, then 14; axe found 0 accessibility violations in all three rounds). Audit C has 40 role-played people (then 48) talk to the intake, with a second model judging each exchange. Each round re-ran all three on the fixed app.

Wrong readings matter more than misses, because a rules reading runs at once. The old rules read 27 of the 413 messages wrongly ("save 10 grand more" became $10); the new rules read 2 wrongly. Admin mode

A hidden screen, /admin, holds 15 sample households in three tiers (comfortable, average, struggling). One click opens a household in the app with no model call, or builds its plan. "Run all" builds all 15; "Save as before", a change to the code, and "Run all" again show every household's verdict, basics paid, count-on, gap and savings at retirement with before and after side by side. A table shows the saved Haiku and gpt-6-luna comparison. It is for checking a fix against the same 15 households.

What the audits missed

After three rounds, I found a broken rule by hand: "Not sure, estimate it" on the mortgage question left the mortgage blank. The rule says a skipped input gets a proposed figure, shown and approved, never a blank. The auditors checked what they were told to check, and the simulated-user judge scored against a hidden true value; a skipped field has none. So the 25 product rules are written down (docs/PRODUCT_RULES.md) with a check for each, 55 tests cover those code can check, and a live audit that clicks every chip is the final gate. Its first run (22 Nov) found 14 rules passing, 10 partial, 1 failing (R1, propose then approve) and no P0; after fixes, the re-check of the 11 that did not pass found 2 pass, 9 partial, 0 fail. Still open: growth rates and plan age are shown but fixed (R6 of the rules list).
