# Enough Lite: research evidence

Compiled 20 Nov 2026. Every figure has a source link. "Checked" means the figure was read on the cited page. "Secondary" means it comes from press coverage of the source, not the source itself. Prices change often; confirm before printing.

## 1. What is new here: existing products

Enough Lite is a household-level answer to one question ("Can I retire when I want, and what should I do first?") that sorts spending into needs (safe), wants (sure) and wishes (open), names three actions, and runs a team of specialist agents. The products below are the closest. I found none that models children and pets in plain words and returns a needs/wants/wishes split. That is a claim about what I could find, not proof that none exists.

Summary from search only; not opened one by one.

Honest summary of novelty: single-assistant AI over a planning engine already exists (Boldin, Copilot), and a multi-agent AI adviser exists (Origin). The defensible difference is the output (needs safe, wants sure, wishes open, plus three actions), plain-word intake that includes family and pets, cited research, and a cost of a cent or two per run. The disadvantages are no linked accounts, no regulation, no human, and no independent accuracy test.

## 3. Why plain answers matter

1. **Planning follows literacy.** Lusardi and Mitchell, using the National Financial Capability Study, found people who answered more literacy questions correctly were much more likely to plan for retirement, and that every group rated its own knowledge highly despite weak scores. [NBER Working Paper 17108, June 2011](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17108) (checked). Older, but it is the standard citation. In the earlier "Big Three" work, only about a third of respondents over 50 got all three questions right, and those who did were almost 10 points more likely to plan (secondary via search, [Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/three-questions-major-implications-financial-well/)).
2. **Literacy is flat and low.** U.S. adults answered 49% of questions correctly on the TIAA Institute-GFLEC P-Fin Index in 2025, the same as 2017; Gen Z got 38%; "comprehending risk" is the weakest area. [TIAA Institute/GFLEC 2025](https://tiaa.org/public/institute/publication/2025/financial-literacy-and-retirement-fluency-in-america) (secondary via search). A 2026 edition says literacy fell to a record low, but I could not read the number (page timed out), so leave it out.
3. **Fewer than half hold a cushion.** Only 46% have three months of emergency savings (NFCS 2024, above). The FINRA release also says the share answering four or more of five literacy questions correctly "held steady" since 2021, with no exact figure on the release page.

## 4. Gaps and cautions

- No government figure found for "never met an adviser" or an aggregate savings gap in dollars. Say so in the report.
- Official pages for Empower, Cleo, Kitces, APA and the NIRS and NFCS PDFs would not load, so those figures are secondary.
- Several vendor prices disagree across sources. The table uses the vendor's own page where it loaded (Boldin, ProjectionLab, MaxiFi, Copilot, Betterment, Vanguard, Fidelity).
- Origin's CFP-exam score and "first SEC-regulated AI adviser" are its own claims.
- Do not claim that no one models pets or children. Say "I found none".
