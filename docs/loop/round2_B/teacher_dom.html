Assumptions
  • All figures are in today's dollars: what money buys now, not the larger sums prices will reach.
  • Savings earn on average 5.0% a year above inflation in stocks and 1.8% in bonds, with ups and downs drawn at random in 2,000 scenarios. These are planning figures, not a forecast.
  • Inflation of 2.5% a year is used only for pensions and annuities without a cost-of-living rise, which lose that much value each year.
  • Until you retire you save about $51,123 a year: $15,000 into a 401(k) or IRA, $36,123 of take-home pay left after your spending, which we assume you save. If that is wrong, change 'Yearly saving' in your answers.
  • Pay stays at today's level until you retire; no raises are assumed.
  • Social Security is a rough estimate from today's pay, for work from about 22 to your retirement age (with fewer than 35 years, the missing years count as zeros). Your own figure is at ssa.gov/myaccount.
  • Social Security starts at 69 for you and at 69 for your partner, each at their own age. A partner with little pay of their own gets half of the other's benefit at full age, less if started earlier.
  • After the first death, expected at the shorter life expectancy, Social Security falls to the larger of your two benefits, about $41,300 a year. Spending stays the same.
  • Tax uses the 2027 federal brackets with the standard deduction (no itemizing) and your state's rates; while you work, 401(k) saving comes off taxable pay and the child tax credit applies. In retirement 85% of Social Security counts as taxable income, the most the law allows, so this errs on the high side.
  • Health cover before Medicare at 65 costs about $10,000 a person a year if you retire earlier (an assumption); out-of-pocket costs follow CMS figures by age.
  • The plan runs to age 96, the age by which there is about a 1 in 4 chance that one of you will still be alive (US population life table, SOA 2024, with future improvement). It is not adjusted for income, and people with more money tend to live longer.
